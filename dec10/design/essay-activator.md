## 2. Key phrases and usage notes by function

### 1. Advantages & Disadvantages
- **Advantages:** advantage (have the advantage of; a distinct advantage over; outweigh its disadvantages), benefit, merit (relative merits; merits and demerits), good point, plus point, the good/great/best thing about, the beauty of X is that
- **Disadvantages:** disadvantage, drawback, downside, bad point
- **Notes:** "the good/great/best thing about" is informal, so keep it out of formal essays. *Merit* is the word to use when weighing options for a decision.

### 2. Agreeing
- **Agree:** agree (with), share somebody's view/concern/fear, subscribe to a view/theory, be of the same opinion, concur (formal), somebody is right / makes a valid point
- **Partly agree:** agree up to a point, broadly agree, there is some truth in
- **Group agreement:** agreement (general agreement that), consensus, common ground, unanimous, widely held view/belief, widely/generally accepted
- **Notes:** a common learner error is "agree someone's opinion" or "agree to someone's opinion". The correct form is *agree with*.

### 3. Aim or Purpose
- **Saying the aim:** aim to do, set out to do (the original aim), in order to, be intended to, be supposed to / be meant to (usually when the aim failed)
- **Nouns:** aim, purpose, objective, goal, target, intention, mission, the point (of), ends (for their own ends = a bad or dishonest aim)

### 4. Approximate / Exact
- **Approximately:** approximately, about, roughly, (somewhere/something) in the region of (large numbers), circa / c. (dates), or more
- **Exactly:** exactly, to be exact (at the end of the sentence), precisely (for emphasis), right (in the middle of…), directly (in front of / behind…)
- **Notes:** *approximately* is more formal and technical than *about*. *Circa* comes from Latin and is often shortened to *c.*.

### 5. Causes
- **To cause:** make, cause, lead to, result in, create, bring about (change), give rise to (formal), generate (interest/support), be responsible for, set off, trigger
- **Caused by:** be caused by, be the result of / result from, arise from, stem from
- **One of several causes:** play a part, be a factor, contribute to, influence
- **Nouns:** cause, factor (key factors), origins, source, root (the root of the problem)
- **Notes:** don't write "make somebody to do". Write *make somebody do*. *Cause* is used mostly for bad outcomes and in scientific or technical description, and it is more formal than *make*. *Result in* is often used for bad outcomes. *Influence* means an indirect effect.

### 6. Certainty & Uncertainty
- **Certain:** certain, sure, convinced, confident, satisfied (that), have no doubt / be in no doubt, without (a) doubt, there is no doubt that / there is no denying/disputing the fact that, certainly, undoubtedly
- **Uncertain:** not certain / uncertain, not sure / unsure, have doubts, be dubious about
- **Notes:** *certain* is more formal and sounds more definite than *sure*. *Not certain / uncertain* is more formal than *not sure*. *Sure*, *convinced*, *confident* and *satisfied* are not used before a noun.

### 7. Comparing & Contrasting
- **Phrases:** compared to/with, by comparison / in comparison, next to / beside, as against / as opposed to, unlike, in contrast / by contrast, in proportion to / in relation to, relative (relative merits)
- **Verbs and collocations:** compare, make/draw a comparison, draw an analogy, draw a parallel, liken X to Y, contrast, make/draw a distinction between
- **Notes:** *draw a comparison* and *draw a distinction* are more formal than the forms with *make*.

### 8. Concluding
- **Concluding:** in conclusion, to summarize / to sum up, X can be summarized as follows, the following conclusion(s) may be drawn, the main conclusion to be drawn is that
- **Restating aims:** the purpose/aim of this [essay] was to…, in this [essay] I have tried/attempted to…
- **Notes:** *To summarize* and *To sum up* open the final paragraph and take a comma or a colon. A conclusion often starts by restating what the essay set out to do, then says what was found.

### 9. Decreasing
- **To decrease:** decrease, go down, decline (formal; also used for standards), fall/drop, plunge/plummet, halve, diminish, dwindle
- **To make decrease:** reduce, lower, bring down, cut, halve, relieve/ease (pain or feelings), alleviate (formal)
- **Nouns:** decrease, reduction, cut, drop/fall, decline
- **Stopping:** level off/out, bottom out
- **Notes:** *decrease* is more formal than *go down*. You can also write that something "is down (to 4%)". *Lower* is more formal than *bring down*.

### 10. Disagreeing
- **Disagree:** disagree / not agree, take issue with (formal, strong), dispute, differ, be divided/split, be mistaken
- **Nouns:** disagreement, dispute, controversy
- **Causing disagreement:** contentious (formal), controversial, divisive

### 11. Effects
- **Nouns:** effect, impact (big and lasting), influence, side effect (often medical), aftereffects, knock-on effect (British English), repercussions (bad, later), implications (likely future effects)
- **Verbs:** affect, be bad for / be good for, impact (on), make a difference, influence
- **Notes:** *effect* is the noun and *affect* is the verb. *Effect*, *impact* and *influence* take *on* (*upon* in formal English). *Impact* as a verb is mainly business English.

### 12. Emphasizing
- **Stressing importance:** I would like to stress that, it should be noted that (formal), it is worth bearing in mind that, crucial, vital/essential
- **One thing more than others:** especially/particularly, in particular, notably (formal), above all, most of all, most importantly/significantly
- **Verbs:** emphasize/stress, highlight, underline/underscore, draw attention to, point up
- **Notes:** *especially* and *particularly* go before a noun phrase or at the start of a clause. *In particular* usually goes after a noun phrase or at the start of a sentence.

### 13. Explaining
- **Phrases:** this means that / which means that, that is, i.e., in other words / to put it another way, to put it simply, specifically
- **Verbs:** explain, give/offer/provide an explanation, set out, go through, outline, expand on, clarify
- **Notes:** use *This means that* at the start of a sentence and *which means that* at the start of a clause. In formal essays, *that is* is better than *i.e.*.

### 14. Giving Examples
- **Giving one:** for example, for instance (slightly less formal), particularly / in particular, be a good/typical/classic example, be a case in point, take / consider (always at the start of the sentence), by way of illustration (formal, for a long example), be shown by, this is true of
- **Many other examples:** such as, including, to name but a few
- **Verbs:** give the example of / give X as an example, cite (formal)
- **Notes:** in essays, *for example* often goes mid-sentence. Write *for example* in full, not *eg*. *Such as* usually has no comma before it, while *for example* and *including* usually do. Avoid *etc* in formal essays and rephrase with *such as*. Never use *eg* and *etc* in the same sentence.

### 15. Giving Exceptions
- **Phrases:** except (for), apart from / aside from, excluding / not including, with the exception of / with one exception (formal), but (after nothing, no one, all and similar words), other than (in negatives), in a few cases / in a small number of cases
- **Noun:** exception
- **Notes:** *except* does not start a sentence, but *except for* and *except in* can. *Excluding* is more formal than *not including*.

### 16. Giving Opinions
- **Your opinion:** in my opinion / in my view, I think that, in this writer's view/opinion (formal, impersonal), it seems to me that, I believe that (strongly held or moral beliefs)
- **Others' opinions:** somebody's opinion/view is that, in somebody's opinion/view, be of the opinion that / take the view that (formal), have/hold an opinion, for [somebody], as far as somebody is concerned, from somebody's point of view
- **Stating opinions:** give/express/voice an opinion, make your views known
- **Notes:** don't write "According to my opinion". Formal essays usually avoid *I* and *me* and use impersonal phrasing instead. The book advises quoting a source in support (e.g. "As X has pointed out…") rather than writing "I think", because a source gives the argument more authority. *Express* and *voice an opinion* are more formal than *give*.

### 17. Giving Reasons
- **Phrases:** because, the reason (for this) is, why, in order to, for, through (why someone succeeded or failed), as a result of, due to / owing to
- **Nouns:** reason, explanation, motive, grounds, argument, rationale, justification, basis
- **Unconvincing reasons:** pretext, excuse
- **Notes:** don't start a sentence with *because* to refer back to the previous sentence; join the two clauses instead. *In order to* is formal, and plain *to* is fine elsewhere.

### 18. Increasing
- **To increase:** increase, rise / go up, grow, double/triple/quadruple, expand, soar, escalate (for bad things)
- **To make increase:** increase, raise, put up, double/triple/quadruple, expand, boost, extend, step up, add to
- **Nouns:** increase, growth, rise, build-up (of something harmful), surge, explosion, boom
- **Stopping:** remain constant / stay the same, peak / reach a peak, level off/out
- **Notes:** *rise* is more formal than *go up*, and you can write "is up by 50%". Don't confuse *rise* (intransitive) with *raise* (transitive): write *raise prices*. *Raise* is more formal than *put up*. Write *an increase in X* but *an increase of 15%*. *Remain constant* is more formal than *stay the same*. In American English *a raise* is a salary increase; British English says *pay rise*. The -ing forms work as adjectives (*the increasing demand*).

### 19. Linking Parts of a Sentence
- **'and'/'also':** and, also, too / as well (as well as), in addition, furthermore / moreover, not to mention
- **'because':** because, due to / owing to, through, thanks to (good outcomes), since / as (British English), in view of (formal), out of (a feeling, e.g. out of curiosity)
- **'but'/'although':** but, although, however / nevertheless, whereas / while, by contrast, yet, even so, in spite of / despite
- **'if':** if, unless, whether or not, otherwise, in case, assuming that, as long as / provided that, on condition that, in the event of/that
- **'in order to':** in order to, so (that), for, with the aim of, with a view to
- **'or':** or, alternatively, on the one hand … on the other (hand)
- **'therefore':** therefore, so, thus, hence, as a result, consequently / as a consequence, for this reason / for these reasons, with the result that, this means that, thereby, accordingly
- **Notes:**
  - Don't start a sentence with *and*.
  - Write *can also*, not "also can". With two negatives use *not … either*, not *also*.
  - *Too* and *as well* go at the end of the clause. *As well as* can come earlier.
  - *In addition*, *furthermore* and *moreover* are very common in essays. *Furthermore* and *moreover* are formal and persuasive.
  - Don't start a sentence with *because* to refer back to the previous one.
  - *However* usually sits mid-sentence between commas, and it can also start a sentence.
  - With *if* about the future, use the present simple ("If it rains…", not "If it will rain").
  - Write *on the one hand*, never "on one hand".
  - Formality scale: *so* < *therefore* < *thus*. *So* comes mid-sentence after a comma. *Therefore* can come mid-sentence or at the start.
  - *Consequently* and *as a consequence* are more formal than *as a result*. *Hence*, *thereby* and *accordingly* are formal.

### 20. Listing & Ordering
- **Ordering:** firstly/first, first of all, secondly/second, thirdly/third, in the first/second/third place (less formal), then/next (processes), in addition, furthermore/moreover, lastly, finally, one last/final point (also *last but not least*, which appears in a model)
- **Introducing a list:** there are several / a number of (reasons…), as follows, the following
- **Notes:** a long run of *Firstly … Secondly … Thirdly … Fourthly* sounds repetitive. Vary it, for example *Firstly … In addition … Furthermore … Last but not least*. A list can follow a colon or a full stop. *Following* works as an adjective (*the following example*) or a noun (*include the following*).

### 21. Making Generalizations
- **Phrases:** most, mostly/mainly/largely, predominantly, almost all / nearly all / virtually all, the majority (of), in general, generally, generally speaking / broadly speaking / as a rule, for the most part, by and large / in the main / on the whole
- **Verb and noun:** generalize, generalization
- **Notes:** write *most people*, not "the most people". *Largely* is more formal than *mostly* or *mainly*. *The majority of* is usually followed by a plural countable noun.

### 22. Problems & Solutions
- **Problems:** problem, issue, challenge, difficulty, trouble, complication, setback, obstacle, pitfall, dilemma, vicious circle
- **Small problems:** teething problems/troubles, hitch, snag
- **Solutions:** solution, answer, way out, cure, remedy, prescription, panacea (formal), quick fix, magic bullet
- **Notes:** *issue* is used for problems that affect society and sounds less negative than *problem*. *Challenge* suggests a problem that is exciting and takes effort to solve. *Hitch*, *snag* and *quick fix* are informal, so avoid them in formal essays. *Cure*, *remedy*, *prescription* and *panacea* are medical metaphors.

### 23. Quoting People
- **Verbs and phrases:** say, write, point out, note / remark (formal), observe, state (definite, authoritative), argue, conclude, claim (the writer doubts it), according to, in the words of
- **Notes:** vary your reporting verbs instead of repeating *say*. "As X points out/notes/states" shows that you agree with the source. *Claim* signals doubt.

### 24. Referring
- **Earlier in the text:** above, previous, preceding, earlier, as has been seen / it has been seen that, the former, the latter
- **Later in the text:** below, the following, as follows, there follows, over page / overleaf, see over, as we shall see
- **Another work:** see, as X says/notes/remarks/points out, cf., op. cit., ibid., loc. cit., pp.
- **Notes:** *above*, *former*, *latter* and *following* each work as adjective or noun. *The following* takes a colon when a list follows immediately. The book glosses the Latin abbreviations: *cf.* = compare, *op. cit.* = in the work already cited, *ibid.* = in the same place, *loc. cit.* = in the same place cited.

### 25. Related / Unrelated
- **Related:** related/connected, linked, (there is a) connection/link, (there is a) correlation, interrelated/interconnected, interdependent, associated (with), be bound up with, go together
- **Relevant to the discussion:** relevant, pertinent (formal), germane (formal), have a bearing on, to the point
- **Not related:** not related / not connected, unrelated/unconnected, there is no relation/connection, separate, discrete (formal), distinct
- **Not relevant:** irrelevant, have nothing to do with, have no bearing on, be beyond/outside the scope of
- **Notes:** *related* can go before a noun but *connected* normally can't. *Not unrelated* means related. *Correlation* is used mainly for statistical or scientific findings.

### 26. Showing & Proving
- **True:** show, prove, demonstrate, X can be seen, be evidence of, be symptomatic of, reveal, confirm, support / back up, corroborate (formal), validate (formal), substantiate (formal)
- **Likely:** suggest, indicate, imply, there is some evidence that, give the impression, lead to the conclusion that
- **Untrue:** disprove, contradict, refute (formal), invalidate (formal), demolish (arguments)
- **Notes:** *prove* and *demonstrate* sound much more definite than *show*. This is useful for teaching hedging and evidence strength.

### 27. Study & Research
- **Verbs:** study, do / carry out / conduct research (into), research, investigate, examine, evaluate, analyse (British) / analyze (American)
- **The work:** study, research, work, experiment, test, trial
- **Written outputs:** essay, paper, dissertation, thesis
- **Results:** results, findings, conclusion
- **Notes:** *conduct research*, *conduct an experiment* and *conduct a test* are more formal than the forms with *do* or *carry out*. *Do research* is more common than *research* as a verb. *Study* is countable (*studies*), but *research* is uncountable, so never write "researches".

### 28. Subjects & Topics
- **Saying what something is about:** about, on, on the subject/topic of, concerning / regarding (formal), with regard to (formal), deal with, be concerned with, focus on, tell the story of
- **Nouns:** subject, topic, theme, issue, question, matter
- **Notes:** write *regarding* or *concerning* on their own, never "regarding to" or "concerning to". *Matter* is vague and general, while *issue* and *question* are specific; write "an important issue", not "a big matter".

### 2b. Quick index of the most teachable warnings
- **Register: informal, avoid in essays:** the good/great/best thing about; hitch; snag; quick fix; i.e. and eg (spell out *that is* and *for example*); etc; the I/me phrases (prefer an impersonal form or a cited source).
- **Formality pairs (more formal listed first):** approximately > about; certain > sure; decrease > go down; rise > go up; raise > put up; lower > bring down; remain constant > stay the same; excluding > not including; draw > make a comparison/distinction; express/voice > give an opinion; consequently / as a consequence > as a result; thus > therefore > so; conduct > do/carry out research; largely > mostly/mainly.
- **Common errors:** agree with (not "agree someone's opinion"); make somebody do (not "make somebody to do"); effect is the noun, affect the verb; rise vs raise; increase in X but increase of 15%; "according to my opinion"; *because* at the start of a sentence to refer back; *and* at the start of a sentence; "also can"; on *the* one hand; "If it will…"; "the most people"; "regarding to"; "researches"; "a big matter".
- **Punctuation:** no comma before *such as*, but a comma before *for example* and *including*; *however* goes between commas mid-sentence; a colon after *the following* or *as follows* before a list; *to sum up* takes a comma or a colon; *so* comes after a comma.

## 3. Essay-writing guidance in this PDF

The PDF has **no essay-writing guide**. There are no pages on essay types, essay structure or planning; the introduction points readers to a separate Writing Handbook that is not included. These parts still give some guidance:

- **Introduction (p.5):** the aim is to vary vocabulary so that essays read better and earn higher grades. Each Activator collects the words for one rhetorical function and is not tied to any subject. Each headword comes with a meaning or usage note and example sentences that students can use as models. The Study Notes cover grammar and formality. A separate Topic Activator covers subject vocabulary, and the Writing Handbook has practice Exercises.
- **Structure advice inside the Activators:**
  - *Conclusions (pp.29–30):* signal the final paragraph with *In conclusion*, *To summarize* or *To sum up*. Restate what the essay set out to do, then summarise the main points or draw a conclusion.
  - *Ordering paragraphs and points (pp.77–79):* introduce a set of points with a framing sentence such as "There are several reasons…" or "…as follows:". Avoid mechanical numbering beyond *firstly* and *secondly*, and vary the connectors.
  - *Using sources (pp.53, 90–91, 94–96):* support claims by citing other writers rather than giving personal opinion, and choose the reporting verb by stance (*state* is authoritative, *claim* doubtful, *point out* agreeing). Use *see*, *cf.* and the Latin citation abbreviations correctly.
  - *Signposting within an essay (pp.92–94):* refer back with *above*, *previous* or *as has been seen*, and forward with *below*, *the following* or *as we shall see*. *The former* and *the latter* refer to two items just mentioned.
  - *Scope (p.101):* use *beyond/outside the scope of* to mark what the essay does not cover.
  - *Hedging and evidence strength (pp.23–25, 102–105):* how strongly a claim is put depends on the verb or phrase chosen. *Prove* and *demonstrate* are strongest, then *show*, then *suggest*, *indicate* and *there is some evidence that*.
- **Learner-facing study skills vocabulary (pp.106–110):** the book defines and distinguishes essay, paper, dissertation and thesis, and results, findings and conclusion.
